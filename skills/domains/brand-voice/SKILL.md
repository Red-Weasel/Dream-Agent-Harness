# Brand voice

Use this when an organisation's writing sounds inconsistent, when a new product needs a voice, or when text must be
rewritten to fit an existing one.

## Method

1. Collect samples the owner considers on-brand (and a few that are not): web pages with `browse`, documents with
   `read_file`, images of printed material with `see`. Note audience and channel for each.
2. Describe what the good samples have in common, with evidence: sentence length, vocabulary level, person (we, you),
   formality, humour, how they handle claims and numbers, words they use and avoid, punctuation habits.
3. Distil three to five voice traits, each as "this, not that" (for example "direct, not blunt"), with one example
   sentence of each side.
4. Add practical rules: preferred terms and banned ones, how to write headings, buttons, error messages and
   apologies, and how the voice shifts by channel (a help article versus a launch post).
5. Test the guide: rewrite two real pieces of text with it, show before and after, and ask the owner which rules need
   adjusting.

## Output

A voice guide saved with `write_file`: who the audience is, the traits with do and don't examples, a word list,
channel notes, and the before-and-after rewrites. Keep it to two pages so people use it.

## Checks

- Each trait is backed by examples from the samples, not invented.
- The rewrites keep every fact of the originals.
- The rules are specific enough that two writers would apply them the same way.

## Pitfalls

- Adjectives everyone claims ("friendly, innovative") with no examples.
- A voice so strong it gets in the way of instructions and error messages.
- Imitating another company's recognisable voice.

Limits: a guide describes choices; the owner decides what the brand should sound like.
